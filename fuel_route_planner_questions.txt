[0:00] 1. How is the route calculated?
SHOW: planner/providers/osrm.py, method route (line 13).
SAY:
  I don't calculate the route myself.
  I make one call to OSRM's public server.
  It gives back the road geometry and the distance.
  The start and the finish are looked up in a local table of 32,000 US places.
  So there's no geocoding call either.

[0:21] 2. How do you know which stations are on the route?
SHOW: planner/services/stations.py, function stations_along (line 106), then the line closeness = index.vectors[chunk] @ sample_vectors.T (line 148).
SAY:
  All 6,600 or so US stations are kept in memory, as numpy arrays.
  A coarse grid narrows them down to the few hundred near the route.
  Then one matrix multiplication gives each station's distance from the route, and its mile marker.
  Anything within five miles counts.
  That takes about fifteen milliseconds for a cross-country trip.
  And it needs no database query.

[0:48] 3. The price file has no coordinates. How are stations placed?
SHOW: README.md, under "How it works": the paragraph that starts "The price file has no coordinates".
SAY:
  The price file only gives a town, and an address like I-80, Exit 223.
  So each station is placed at the centre of its town.
  Most come from the Census place file.
  For the 313 stations whose towns the Census doesn't list, I did a one-off OpenStreetMap lookup, and committed the results.
  That's a few miles off the real exit, and I say so in the limitations.

[1:18] 4. How are the fuel stops chosen?
SHOW: planner/services/optimizer.py: the docstring at the top, then plan_fuel_stops (line 49).
SAY:
  It minimises the fuel bill, plus a fixed cost for every stop.
  That cost is five dollars by default.
  When stops are free, a greedy rule is optimal.
  If a cheaper station is in range, buy just enough to reach it.
  Otherwise, fill up.
  With a stop cost, I use dynamic programming, over the station and the fuel on arrival.
  Both are checked against brute force on random routes.

[1:49] 5. Why is there a cost per stop at all?
SHOW: Postman, the request "Cheapest possible fuel bill (stop_cost 0)": fuel_stops is 14.
SAY:
  Because the cheapest plan isn't one anyone would drive.
  From New York to Los Angeles it's fourteen stops, to save about three dollars.
  At five dollars a stop, that becomes seven stops.
  And the fuel costs less than half a percent more.

[2:09] 6. What is cached?
SHOW: planner/services/trip.py, function plan_trip (line 272), then the line cached = _cache_get_many([route_key, stops_key]) (line 300).
SAY:
  Two things, for an hour, in Redis.
  The first is the route made ready for planning.
  That's its line, plus the stations matched to it, about 110 kilobytes.
  The second is the stops chosen for each set of settings, a kilobyte or two.
  A repeat reads both in one round trip, and makes no routing call.
  If I change a setting, the route is reused and only the stops are worked out again.

[2:41] 7. How fast is it, and how many routing calls does it make?
SHOW: Postman, "meta" at the bottom of a response: routing_api_calls and elapsed_ms.
SAY:
  One routing call for a new trip, and none for a repeat.
  A new cross-country trip takes between a third of a second and a second.
  Nearly all of that is the routing call.
  A repeat takes about ten milliseconds.
  And each plan is three SQL queries, about two milliseconds together.

[3:04] 8. What does the total fuel cost mean?
SHOW: Postman, "summary" in a response: total_fuel_cost and fuel_used_cost.
SAY:
  It's the fuel bought on the way.
  The vehicle starts with a full tank by default.
  So there's a second figure, fuel used cost.
  That one also counts the fuel it set off with.
  And a request can say it starts with less.

[3:24] 9. What are its limits?
SHOW: README.md, the "Limitations" section.
SAY:
  Station positions are town centres, not exact exits.
  The detour to reach a station isn't counted.
  And the public OSRM server routes for cars, and has no uptime guarantee.
  Those are in the README, with a few more.
